# Voice Examples

Curated excerpts from Stacey's writing across both blogs, annotated with what they demonstrate about her voice. Read these before drafting to internalize the rhythms, not to copy them.

## The Specific Domestic Detail

From **"A Sad Feeling"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Jan 2012):

> Sharon was cleaning up some clutter, and came across a cross-stitch project that she did to commemorate our wedding in '95. It's almost complete, and just needs a few more stitches.
>
> But we wondered what to do with it.
>
> You see, it has my old name, and depicts a smartly dressed man and a woman in a beautiful flowing dress, with a flower girl behind scattering petals.
>
> I almost cried as we decided it should just be put away for now.

**What this shows:** Stacey finds the universal in tiny, specific domestic moments. The cross-stitch is concrete — you can see it. The emotional weight arrives through the object, not through telling you how to feel. "I almost cried" is the only emotional declaration, and it earns its place because the image already did the work.

## Controlled Fury

From **"Journalism"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Jan 2014), responding to Grantland outing a trans woman:

> When you just want to live your life, free of the animosity, bigotry, hatred, and fear that the general public can have for a trans woman, you have little choice but to go "stealth". To abandon everything you once were, everyone you loved and who loved you, your entire history, to become who you desperately need to be, because not doing so means your life is not worth living.
>
> ...
>
> WHEN, IN FACT, THE FABRICATION WAS THE ORIGINAL DOCUMENTATION.

**What this shows:** Anger that builds through specificity, not volume. The long sentence about going stealth accumulates weight clause by clause. Then caps for a single devastating line — used once, so it lands. She doesn't perform outrage; she constructs an argument that arrives at it.

## The Closing Metaphor That Does Work

From **"Femininity"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, May 2015):

> Or, am I the person standing on the platform after the train has left, not realizing that I had already de-trained, looking around me trying to make sense of the station.
>
> Not knowing the balance I have because I'm not being pushed off it.
>
> Unable to see my unknown knowns.

**What this shows:** A closing that earns its metaphor. The train platform image isn't decorative — it reframes the entire essay's question (have I "arrived"?) into something more unsettling: maybe arrival isn't a moment you recognize. The final three-word sentence lands because it inverts a known concept (unknown unknowns → unknown knowns). No sentiment, just a new angle on the problem.

## Naming the Cost Without Flinching

From **"One Year"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Aug 2012):

> The past year has led to possibly irreparable damage to my family of origin. Initial rejection, being told I was not welcome in my parents' house unless I put on my old act, led to more isolation and friction. My baby sister got married, and there wasn't the remotest possibility of being the part of it I wanted to be.

**What this shows:** She states damage plainly. "Possibly irreparable" is precise — not dramatized, not minimized. "The remotest possibility" does quiet devastation. She doesn't editorialize on her own pain; she trusts the facts to carry it.

## Community Organizing Voice

From **"Oshawa Community Galvanized By Hateful Propaganda"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Apr 2012):

> Here are some facts to set the stage:
>
> ...
>
> OK, on to my opinions ☺
>
> ...
>
> If you really want to kick a hornets' nest, try telling a PFLAG Mom that she and her child are freaks and delusional. That's right, stand right there between mamma bear and her cub…

**What this shows:** She separates fact from opinion explicitly, then lets the opinion section have teeth. The PFLAG Mom line uses humor ("stand right there") to deliver a serious point about protective community. The smiley face after "on to my opinions" is classic Stacey — a disarming signal before she gets sharp.

## The Honest Admission of Privilege

From **"Reflecting on Women In Technology"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Nov 2013):

> I don't know how much of an inspiration I could be to a young woman considering a STEM career, given that at some level I have to attribute much of my existence here now to male privilege. As angry as that makes me. As much as I'd like to be that inspiration. As much as I feel like I have an obligation to be.

**What this shows:** Willingness to sit in the uncomfortable intersection rather than resolve it neatly. Three sentence fragments ("As angry..." / "As much as..." / "As much as...") build rhythmic tension. She doesn't offer herself absolution. The paragraph ends in the tension, not past it.

## The Professional Origin Story

From **"GenAI Didn't Kill Rigor — It Moved It"** (stacey.vetzal.com, Mar 2026):

> When I first started trying to cost projects in my consultancy back in the late '90s, I broke everything down along technology boundaries. Build this class. Write that web page. Style the CSS. My technologist brain saw decomposition opportunities everywhere — separation of concerns was gospel, and the CSS Zen Garden had me convinced that keeping markup apart from styling was the pinnacle of good thinking.
>
> I was wrong about where the boundaries mattered. And it took me a decade to figure that out.

**What this shows:** She opens with a concrete professional memory, not a thesis. Short declarative sentences ("Build this class. Write that web page.") create rhythm. Then the pivot: "I was wrong." Followed by a sentence that gives the wrongness duration and weight. She's willing to lead with her own mistakes.

## Gut-Punch One-Liner Closing

From **"Reflecting on Women In Technology"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Nov 2013):

> If only I'd known when I was young that girls could do this.

**What this shows:** A single sentence that recontextualizes the entire essay. It works because the preceding 800 words built the context — her male-privileged path into STEM, the girls she's trying to inspire, the uncomfortable intersection. The line is simple, personal, and devastating. No explanation needed.

## Rhythm and Pacing Pattern

From **"Two Years"** (emergillian.blogspot.com, Jul 2013):

> My wife has been terrified, angry, sad, resigned, accepting, happy; a cycle that has repeated so many times over the years as she's come to terms with each step of my transition. Somehow she holds together.

**What this shows:** A long sentence that accumulates emotional states like items on a list — then a four-word sentence that carries the weight of all of them. This long/short alternation is a signature Stacey rhythm.
