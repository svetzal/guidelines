# Persuasive Reframe

Writing that judges its reader doesn't persuade its reader. When prose challenges someone's
worldview head-on — indicts their practices, grades their work, tells them what they're doing
wrong — they don't lean in and reconsider. They go on the back foot, tune out, and file the
piece as not worth listening to. Every judgemental sentence spends credibility the ideas needed.

The goal of this skill is not to weaken claims. It's to remove judgement of *people* so the
strength of the *idea* can land. A reader holding a different view should finish the piece
feeling invited to consider an idea that can live in tandem with their own — not told to
surrender theirs.

This is a matter of humility (we don't know the whole world's context), practicality (defensive
readers don't engage), and respect (skilled people usually have reasons for what they do).

## Core principles

1. **Invite, don't indict.** An indictment ("Your agent guidance is an untested dependency")
   makes the reader the defendant before the first paragraph. An invitation offers the same idea
   as something you found and they might find useful. Nobody appointed us judge of the world's
   practices; write like a colleague sharing a discovery, not a verdict.

2. **Speak from experience, not from the bench.** Ground claims in first person — what I tried,
   what we noticed, what happened in our projects. "I started treating my agent guidance like a
   dependency, and here's what broke" carries the same idea as the indictment, with evidence
   instead of authority.

3. **Offer ideas that live in tandem.** Frame the new idea as an *and*, not an *instead*. The
   reader's current practice probably works for reasons you can't see; your idea can sit
   alongside it. Prose that demands replacement triggers defence of the old thing rather than
   curiosity about the new one.

4. **Scope claims to what you actually know.** "Everyone", "nobody", "always", and unqualified
   second-person generalizations claim knowledge you don't have. "In my projects", "often",
   "the teams I've worked with" are honest — and honesty is more persuasive than sweep.

5. **Respect the reader's competence.** Write for a skilled practitioner who does it differently
   and has thought about why. Never explain their own job to them, and never imply that
   disagreement equals not having thought about it.

6. **Curiosity over confrontation.** Real questions open doors ("what would change if we could
   test this?"). Rhetorical questions that are accusations in costume ("why are you still doing
   X?") are indictments and read as such.

## Judgement tells — what to sweep for

- **Second-person indictments**, especially in titles: "Your X is <bad thing>", "You're doing X
  wrong".
- **Grading adjectives** aimed at the reader's work: untested, broken, naive, sloppy, lazy,
  outdated, wrong.
- **Universal quantifiers**: everyone, nobody, always, never, "the industry", "most teams" (when
  you haven't measured most teams).
- **Imperative scolding**: "Stop doing X", "It's time to admit…".
- **Accusatory rhetorical questions**: "Why are you still…?", "When will we finally…?"
- **Verdict framing**: sentences whose grammatical subject is the reader or their work, and whose
  predicate is a judgement.

## Reframe moves

| Judgemental | Reframed |
| --- | --- |
| "Your Agent Guidance Is an Untested Dependency" | "What Happened When I Started Testing My Agent Guidance" |
| "Most teams are doing code review wrong" | "A code review habit that changed how my reviews land" |
| "Stop writing brittle prompts" | "What made my prompts less brittle" |
| "Nobody measures this, and it shows" | "I hadn't measured this either — here's what I found when I did" |

The recurring moves behind the table:

- **Verdict → experience.** Replace the judgement of the reader with a report of what you did
  and what you observed.
- **Second person → first person.** Move the subject of the sentence from "you/your" to "I/we"
  wherever the predicate carries any negative charge.
- **Command → outcome.** Instead of telling the reader to change, show the outcome that made
  you change.
- **Universal → scoped.** Shrink the claim to the territory you've actually walked.

## What this is not

Don't hedge ideas into mush. Conviction about the *idea* stays — be vivid, concrete, and
specific about what you found and why it matters. The reframe removes judgement of people, not
strength of argument. "This might possibly sometimes help some people" is a different failure,
not a fix.

## The back-foot test

Before finishing any title or opening line, run this check: imagine a skilled, thoughtful
practitioner who does it differently reading it cold. Is their first reaction *curiosity* ("huh,
what did they find?") or *defence* ("who are you to say that?")? If it's defence, reframe before
shipping — the rest of the piece won't get read.
